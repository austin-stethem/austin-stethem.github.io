The Enterprise Scenario (Step 1 of 5) Before we can execute defensive pricing strategies across the distribution network, we must first understand our exact geographic exposure. Relying on static delivery radii leaves operations blind to regional competitor density and market cannibalization. Our first objective is to build a spatial intelligence engine that maps these threats in real-time, pinpointing exactly where our market leverage is strong and where our territories are highly vulnerable.

1. The Problem: From Geometric Guesswork to Predictive Capture

In high-velocity distribution, defining sales territories with a static "20-mile radius" is a recipe for margin erosion. A 20-mile delivery in gridlocked downtown Atlanta destroys logistics profitability, while a 20-mile route in rural Iowa represents a highly profitable, captive market. To move from descriptive reporting to prescriptive revenue capture, I replaced this static guesswork with a dynamic Geospatial Intelligence Engine.

By programmatically harvesting and mapping thousands of competitor locations against our internal Hub & Spoke network, the engine exposed our exact territorial blind spots. This allowed operations leadership to instantly visualize market friction, identifying precisely where our routes were exposed to competitor cannibalization, and where we had the geographic leverage to safely raise prices.

2. Machine Learning: Translating Maps into Margin

A map is just a picture until you attach revenue to it. To translate this geography into an actionable pricing strategy, I engineered a POS Gravity Engine. By extracting 12 months of ERP invoice data and geocoding thousands of Customer Ship-To addresses, the Python engine calculated the exact mathematical "Distance Decay" of our revenue—proving exactly how far customers were willing to travel, or wait for delivery, before defecting to a competitor.

I then fed these decay curves into an unsupervised K-Means Machine Learning Algorithm (via scikit-learn). Instead of relying on subjective opinions from regional managers, the algorithm mathematically clustered the branch network into 5 distinct Strategic Topology Tiers based on their actual friction profiles.

What the tier model covers, and what it deliberately does not Seventy of eighty locations produce a decay profile, and the ten that do not are almost all excluded on purpose. A trade-area model describes how revenue falls away with drive time from a retail counter — which is a meaningless question to ask of a distribution centre, a machine shop doing engine rebuilds, or a specialty division selling industrial coatings to a national account list. Those locations sit in the same field as the branches and are structurally different businesses, so the filtering that keeps them out is a modelling decision rather than a gap. One location in that set is a genuine miss: a retail branch whose name is transposed between the location directory and the transaction extract, so an exact-match join never finds it. That is a single small branch rather than an eighth of the network, and the normalisation work built to catch that class of failure is the subject of a separate case study.

This fundamentally changed how the enterprise priced its catalog. We now knew with mathematical certainty which branches operated as "Tier 1: Rural Monopolies" (capable of absorbing aggressive, network-funding price increases) and which were "Tier 5: Hyper-Dense Urban" (requiring strict, defensive margin floors to survive block-by-block warfare without destroying volume).

Making an Unsupervised Model Answer to a Business Question

K-Means returns cluster labels, not tiers. The integers it assigns carry no order and no meaning — cluster 3 is not between cluster 2 and cluster 4 in any sense — and shipping them to a pricing coordinator as "Tier 3" would be presenting noise as hierarchy. Two steps sit between the raw output and something a pricing decision can rest on.

The clustering found a business model, not just a geography Four of the five tiers separate retail locations by how tightly their revenue concentrates near the counter. The fifth does something different: it isolates a small number of operations whose revenue is majority long-haul — more than half of it originating beyond a hundred miles. Those are not remote branches. They are businesses that do not serve a trade area at all, and the algorithm separated them from every retail location without being told they were different in kind. That is the argument for an unsupervised method over a rules-based one: a rule written by hand would have sorted on geography, because geography is what the analyst was thinking about.

The clusters are ordered against a business axis. After fitting, each cluster's mean dependency on its core five-mile band is computed, and the clusters are sorted on that single figure. The least core-dependent becomes Tier 1, the most becomes Tier 5. The result is an ordinal scale where the number itself carries the strategic meaning: a higher tier is measurably more exposed to a competitor across the street, and that is what licenses a tighter margin floor. Feature scaling happens before fitting, because K-Means is a distance-based algorithm and an unstandardised band with a wider spread would silently dominate the geometry.

What the Tiers Cannot See: A Shape Test

Clustering on six distance bands sorts locations well, but it has a blind spot that only becomes visible once you know what some of the locations actually are. Three quite different operations can carry a similar share of revenue at long range, and the tier model will place them together:

The middle bands are the discriminator, not the tail. Long-haul share is the obvious thing to sort on and it cannot tell these apart. What separates them is whether the five-to-fifty mile range holds anything. A hub's collapses to near zero — in one production case to under one percent — because a hub serves its own town across a counter and everything else by freight, with no reason to serve the ring in between. A remote branch has no such gap; its revenue tapers.

So each location carries a shape classification alongside its tier, computed from the ratio of middle-band share to the two ends. It is a deliberately simple rule rather than a second clustering pass, for two reasons: it is auditable by anyone who can read a percentage, and it needs to work on the handful of locations where the tier model has too few examples to learn the distinction itself. The interactive map above reports it in each hub popup.

Why a hand-written rule beats the algorithm here Unsupervised methods learn from density. Where a category has two members, there is no density to learn from, and the clustering will attach them to whichever centroid is least wrong — grouping a hub-store with a distribution operation because both have a long tail, despite their local business being completely different. A named rule encoding what somebody knows about how the operations work is the right tool for the sparse case, and the clustering remains the right tool for the other sixty-eight.

Every assignment carries a confidence figure, and it is derived rather than asserted. For each branch the model records the distance to its assigned cluster centre and the distance to the next-nearest, and reports one minus their ratio. A branch sitting midway between two centres scores near zero; a decisively placed one approaches 100%. Anything below 20% is flagged as borderline and excluded from automated action.

Why the borderline flag matters more than the tiers do Roughly one branch in eight does not classify cleanly. Those are the branches where a rural profile shades into a regional one, and where an automated price increase justified by "Tier 1 can absorb it" would be justified by a coin flip. A model that reports which of its own outputs it is unsure about can be trusted with the rest; one that returns five confident tiers for every branch cannot.

The interactive model above runs this same calculation. The confidence figure in each hub popup is computed from that hub's own decay profile against the cluster centres, not attached as decoration — which is why some hubs report a borderline status and why a hub occasionally classifies into a neighbouring tier rather than the one its geography would suggest.

3. Enterprise Ecosystem: Scaling the Architecture

This geospatial engine was not a single-use script; it was architected as a foundational data ecosystem. Using this exact Python & SQL backbone, I expanded the architecture to solve massive operational inefficiencies across the enterprise:

  • M&A Poaching & Cannibalization: Modeled regional overlaps between merging competitors to identify "cross-street" store closures, allowing our outside sales reps to preemptively target and poach abandoned commercial accounts.
  • Greenfield Expansion (Whitespace): Identified high-density VIO corridors completely void of our distribution hubs, generating data-backed expansion targets for executive leadership.
  • Logistics & Fleet Optimization: Modified the engine to map physical delivery vectors, identifying severe overlapping territories to eliminate margin waste on fuel, driver overtime, and fleet wear-and-tear.
  • Sales Territory Calibration: Visualized actual POS revenue vectors against assigned salesman routes to expose under-serviced regions and rebalance rep workloads.

Cross-Functional Enterprise Adoption

While initially architected to solve regional pricing elasticity, the underlying spatial engine was designed to be highly modular. Following an executive demo, the foundation was rapidly scaled across multiple departments. The core architecture was adapted to generate logistics spider-maps for freight and route optimization, competitor density models for Sales & Marketing, and Greenfield/M&A threat-monitoring dashboards utilized directly by the Company President. The objective was not to build a single-use script, but a flexible, scalable enterprise tool.
