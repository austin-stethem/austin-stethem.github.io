The Extract Layer

Principal Engineer Austin Sonny Stethem

1. Every Model Starts With a Query Nobody Reads

Where this sits in the track The four steps before this one deal with data that is wrong in visible ways — inventory that no longer exists, an entity with two names, a customer in the wrong place, an account with three owners. This step deals with data that is wrong because of a decision somebody made once, correctly, and never wrote down.

Every dashboard in this portfolio, and every model behind them, begins with a transaction extract. The elasticity tiers, the decay curves, the merger closure forecast, the clawback audit — all of them consume the output of a query, and none of them can tell whether that query was right.

That query is where margin gets defined. Not measured, defined. It decides what counts as revenue, what counts as cost, which rows belong in the population, and over what window. Change any of those and every downstream number changes with it, silently, because nothing downstream knows the definition moved.

2. Gross Profit Is a Definition

This is my layer. Every exclusion below is a call I made and can defend line by line, which rows count as revenue, which negatives are customer credits against internal transfers against corruption, what cost means when the same part carries three of them. None of it is visible in the output, and all of it changes the answer. When a number leaves this layer wrong, nothing downstream catches it: the dashboards render, the charts look right, and the decision gets made on it anyway.

Margin is computed at extract time as revenue less store-intake cost: the tier the branch itself acquires at, not the warehouse's landed cost beneath it or the list price above it. That choice is deliberate and it is the reason the margin figures in this portfolio reconcile to the price ladder instead of floating free of it.

Choosing a different basis produces a different, equally defensible, entirely incompatible number:

Cost basis What it measures Why it is the wrong basis for pricing work
Warehouse landed cost Enterprise margin, distribution centre through to customer Correct for consolidated reporting, useless for a branch conversation. It credits the branch with margin earned upstream and hides whether the branch's own pricing is working.
Store intake cost What the branch earns on what the branch sells The basis used here. It is the cost the branch actually faces, so a margin floor expressed against it is a floor a branch manager can act on.
Replacement cost Margin on what the item costs to restock today The right basis during a cost shock and the wrong one for routine reporting. Mixing the two is exactly the error the inventory revaluation work exists to correct.

None of these is more true than the others. The failure is not choosing wrong; it is choosing without recording the choice, so that two reports built a year apart on different bases get compared as though they measure the same thing.

When the Cost Column Is Not Populated for Every Line

Choosing a basis is the easy part. The harder problem is that the chosen cost is not recorded on every transaction, and the arithmetic does not notice.

In a legacy pricing structure, not every part carries a price at every tier. Some have no jobber tier, some no dealer tier, some no special rate — any combination can be absent, because the ladder accreted over decades instead of being designed. Add pass-through lines, where product moves from a warehouse to a branch with no markup applied, and accounts running negotiated cost-price rules that set their basis outside the standard ladder entirely, and a meaningful share of transaction lines simply have no value in the cost column.

Now consider how margin is calculated over a group of those lines:

Line Revenue Cost  
Line 1100.0065.00costed
Line 280.0052.00costed
Line 3120.00 — no cost on file
Line 460.0039.00costed

An aggregate function ignores empty values. So the revenue side totals 360 across all four lines while the cost side totals 156 across only three. The two sums are taken over different sets of rows, and subtracting one from the other reports a 56.7% margin where the costed lines actually returned 35.0%.

Why this one is worse than the exclusions

A wrong population produces a number that is wrong by a constant. This produces a number whose error scales with the share of uncosted revenue in each group — so two locations selling different product mixes report different margins for reasons that have nothing to do with pricing. It looks like performance variation. It is a data gap wearing performance variation's clothes.

The consequence reaches further than reporting. Any ranking of products or accounts by profitability places uncosted lines at the top, because a missing cost computes as no cost. An assortment recommendation built on that ranking will systematically favour precisely the items whose cost nobody recorded.

The Fix Is the Same Pattern Used for Geography

The repair is not to guess a cost, and not to drop the uncosted lines. It is to make the gap a visible quantity and to compute margin only over the rows where both sides exist:

  • Total revenue — every line, unchanged. This is what the business actually sold.
  • Costed revenue, revenue on lines that carry a cost. The correct denominator for a margin.
  • Gross profit — costed revenue less cost, so both sides span the same rows.
  • Uncosted revenue: the remainder, reported as its own column, not silently absorbed.

That is structurally identical to the data-gap column carried in the trade-area model, where revenue that could not be placed geographically is held aside instead of being renormalised into the distance bands. The same discipline, applied to a different missing dimension: state the denominator, and report what fell outside it. A margin computed over an unknown subset of rows is not a measurement, and the only way to know which it is happens to be a column that most extracts never carry.

3. Four Exclusions, Six Points of Margin

The population is as consequential as the arithmetic. Four categories of row sit in the source data and belong in almost no analysis, and each was excluded because leaving it in produced a specific, traceable distortion.

The exclusion that matters most

Labour and service lines carry revenue and no parts cost. Computing margin as revenue minus cost therefore returns 100% for every one of them; and blending that into a parts margin inflates the reported figure by several points. A model calibrated on the inflated number sets its floors too low, and does so confidently.

Three kinds of negative revenue, indistinguishable to a SUM

Exclusion is not only about categories of row. It is also about telling apart records that look identical to an aggregate function and mean completely different things. Negative revenue is the clearest case: a transaction extract contains at least three species of it, each requiring a different response, and a single WHERE revenue > 0 handles all three wrongly.

Species What it looks like Correct treatment
Customer credits and returns many rows, small values Keep them. They are real transactions and netting them out is how revenue is actually earned. Filtering them inflates every account's apparent value.
Internal warehouse accounts few rows, large values Exclude as internal, not as an error. A distribution warehouse appears in the extract carrying a branch name, but it is not a retail location — it holds a small number of wholesale accounts at enormous individual values, and its negatives are transfers, not customer credits.
Corruption very few rows, absurd values Investigate, and never silently drop. A single account carrying a negative value in the billions is not a return, an adjustment, or a rounding artefact. It is a defect, and quarantining it without raising it means it stays in the source and reappears next quarter.

The magnitudes involved make the point better than the taxonomy does. In one extract, legitimate credits across nearly two thousand rows netted to roughly negative ten million, entirely plausible for the volume. Two corrupt rows, from a total of tens of thousands, carried a combined value of negative fifteen and a half billion. An unguarded sum over that file therefore reports the network as having earned negative revenue for the year.

That is a useful failure precisely because it is absurd. The dangerous version is the same defect two orders of magnitude smaller — large enough to move a branch's reported margin by several points, small enough that nobody's instinct flags it. Which is the argument for reconciling the extract to a known total on every run instead of reading the output and deciding whether it looks about right.

A warehouse is not a branch

The internal-account case deserves its own note, because it is a classification failure, not a data-quality one. A distribution warehouse sits in the same field as the retail branches and is structurally a different business: on the order of a hundred wholesale accounts averaging several hundred thousand dollars each, against a retail branch's thousands of accounts averaging a few thousand.

Nothing is wrong with either record. But any model that treats the two as peers: a branch ranking, a trade-area decay curve, a per-account average — produces a result driven almost entirely by the warehouse. It is the same lesson as the negative revenue: the extract will happily hand you both, and the query is the only place the distinction can be made once for everybody.

Partial filtering is worse than none

The drift is not monotonic, and that is the part worth sitting with. With every row included the figure reads 36.72%, inflated by labour. Exclude labour alone and it falls to 31.24% — now understated, because internal transfers running at roughly five percent margin dominate what is left. Only with the full exclusion set does it settle at 35.00%.

So an analyst who correctly identifies the labour problem and fixes only that produces a number further from the truth than the one they started with, and has every reason to believe they have improved it. The distortions do not cancel in any predictable direction, which is why the population has to be defined as a set instead of patched one exclusion at a time.

5. The Schema Does Not Describe Itself

A system that has run for decades accumulates fields whose names stopped matching their contents long before anyone currently employed arrived. A column named for a payment-sequencing attribute holds the state code. The address fields are split so that the second one carries the city, not a second address line. A field whose name suggests a sort order is, in practice, a geographic key.

None of this is documented, and none of it is discoverable by reading the schema. It is discoverable by pulling a hundred rows, looking at them, and noticing that the column labelled one thing contains two-letter state abbreviations. Every extract in this portfolio depends on somebody having done that, and on the aliases in the query being right — because an alias that is wrong produces a column that looks correct and contains the wrong thing.

This is the least transferable skill in the whole portfolio and the hardest to fake. It is also the reason a competent analyst dropped into a legacy environment is unproductive for months: the queries are easy and knowing what the fields actually hold is not.

An exclusion that silently was not applying

One of the filters above spent a period not working, because of a missing conjunction in the clause that applied it. The query ran. It returned rows. The rows looked correct. The only symptom was a blended margin that read a few points better than anybody's instinct said it should, which is the kind of discrepancy that gets rationalised instead of investigated, because the number was flattering.

It surfaced when the figure was checked against a known parts-only baseline. That is the general lesson: the way you find an extract-layer error is by reconciling to something computed a different way, because the extract itself will never tell you.

6. From Query to Service

The extract is the first stage of a chain that ends in a browser. Geocoding, distance banding and clustering run over the extract output in Python. A small service layer then assembles five independent sources: the location census, the branch directory, the gravity matrix, the topology tiers and the account-level profiles, into a single payload the front end consumes.

That assembly is deliberately fault-tolerant. Each source is loaded independently and a missing one degrades its own section instead of taking down the response. A dashboard that renders with an empty analytics panel is diagnosable; one that returns a server error at nine in the morning is a fire.

The Open Problem: Cost Is an Allocation Choice

Everything above treats store-intake cost as a given. It is not. Freight arrives on a mixed pallet carrying items of different weights, values and volumes, and the freight has to be spread across them somehow. Allocate by weight and heavy low-value items absorb the cost. Allocate by value and expensive items do. Allocate by cube and bulky ones do. The three methods produce materially different per-item costs from identical invoices.

Which means the profitability of an individual line is partly an artefact of an accounting convention chosen upstream, and any model ranking items by margin inherits that convention without knowing it exists. Making that allocation explicit, and testing how much of the item-level margin ranking survives changing it, is the piece of work I would do next; and it is the reason this page closes the track instead of opening it.

End of the Foundations Track

Five failure modes: inventory that no longer exists, an entity with two names, a customer in the wrong place, an account with three owners, and a cost that is a convention. Each one silently corrupts everything built on top of it.
