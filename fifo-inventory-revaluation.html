1. Situation & Task: The Inflationary Lag

The Enterprise Scenario (Step 5 of 5) Our statistical A/B tracking (Step 4) confirms our new price points are holding firm in the market without sacrificing volume. The last part of the cycle is the one with the shortest window. Because our supply chain teams secured 60 days of inventory before the 15% cost spike hit, and we decoupled our retail price from our physical receiving docks, we are selling cheap stock at the newly elevated retail tiers. I modelled this EBITDA windfall.

A vendor announces a 10% increase. Most pricing systems then wait: retail stays where it is until the higher-cost stock arrives at the dock and posts to the ledger. That lag is the whole subject of this page.

That latency costs real money. A mature distribution network often holds 60 to 90 days of Quantity On Hand (QOH) purchased at the old, cheaper cost. I recommended decoupling the retail pricing trigger from the physical receiving dock, so that a price reflects what the next unit will cost rather than what the last one did.

2. Action: Pricing on Replacement Cost

The decision here is which cost a price is allowed to reference, and it belongs to accounting as much as to pricing, which is why it needed agreeing rather than implementing. Average cost is the intuitive answer and the wrong one: it lags, so during a rising market every price it produces is anchored to inventory already sold. Replacement cost is forward-looking and harder to defend in a month when nothing moved. I argued for replacement cost and for stating the windfall as avoided cost, not earned profit, because the second framing is the one that survives a question from a controller.

I transitioned the organizational pricing mechanism away from Average Cost and strictly onto Replacement Cost. By architecting this business logic into the ERP, the system instantly triggers a retail markup the moment a vendor price increase is announced — regardless of what is sitting on our shelves.

Systems Integration (The Ecosystem): In reality, vendor cost increases are never a flat percentage across all SKUs. As demonstrated in my Algorithmic Margin Defense and Stochastic Optimization models, cost shocks vary wildly by item. Those engines handle the micro-level elasticity, protecting KVIs and finding margin on elastic items, to ultimately calculate a Weighted Average Cost Spike for the portfolio. It is that finalized, blended deficit that feeds into this macro-level FIFO model.

Because accounting strictly follows FIFO (First-In, First-Out), we are now selling inventory that was bought cheaply, but charging the customer the new, inflated market rate. This gap between the old physical cost and the new retail price creates a temporary, massive spike in margin: The EBITDA Windfall.

4. Result: Bottom-Line P&L Impact

Turning Inflation into a Revenue Center

By shifting the organization's logic from Average Cost to Replacement Cost, the Pricing Department ceased to be a reactive administrative function and became an active revenue center.

No model was needed here. What was needed was an understanding of how the ledger and the loading dock disagree, and the discipline to measure the gap between them instead of treating it as noise. Be exact about what that gap is. It is not profit created by raising a price. It is cost avoided because the stock was bought before the increase. Measured against the price rise it looks far larger than it is; measured against replacement cost you get the real figure, and it lasts precisely as long as the old-cost inventory.
