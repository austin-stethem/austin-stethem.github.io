1. Finding Whitespace Is the Easy Half

Where this sits in the track Step 1 built the competitive footprint and used it defensively — who is near our accounts, and what happens when they consolidate. The same asset inverted answers a different question: where is there demand that nobody is serving.

Whitespace analysis has an obvious first move. Take vehicle population by geography, subtract the areas already inside somebody's service coverage, and what remains is demand without a supplier. That produces a map with holes in it, and the holes look like opportunities.

The problem is that a map with holes in it is not a business case. Every uncovered corridor is uncovered for a reason, and the most common reason is that it does not contain enough addressable spend to support a building. A whitespace map that cannot distinguish a corridor worth $2M a year from one worth $300,000 will send you to both, and the second one will lose money for a decade.

So the analysis has to run in two stages: find the gaps, then test each gap against the cost of standing a branch in it. The second stage is the one that produces a decision.

2. Subtracting Coverage From Demand

Coverage is not a radius. A branch does not serve a circle; it serves the area a delivery vehicle can reach and return from inside a working window, which is a drive-time contour, and drive time converts to distance very differently in a metro than on open highway. The same scaling used in the trade-area model applies here: an urban location covers a tighter footprint in miles than a rural one covering the same time band.

Coverage subtraction therefore runs against contours rather than circles, and it counts competitor coverage as well as our own. A corridor already served by two national chains is not whitespace; it is a fight. Genuine whitespace is demand outside everyone's reach.

4. What the Model Is For

The value is not the list of viable corridors. It is that the same tool produces the list of rejected ones, with a number attached to each rejection. A corridor carrying 9,500 uncovered vehicles looks like an opportunity on a map and fails on contribution at every realistic capture rate — and being able to show that in a sentence is worth more than another green pin.

It also makes the assumptions arguable, which is the point of putting them on sliders. Nobody is going to agree with a single capture-rate estimate. What they can agree on is a threshold: at what capture rate does this corridor become fundable, and is that rate plausible given who else is nearby. That converts a forecast into a conversation about one number.

The Honest Limitation

Vehicle population is a proxy for demand, not a measure of it. A corridor full of vehicles whose owners service them at dealerships, or a corridor whose shops are locked into a competitor's programme agreement, will register as demand and deliver none. The model sizes the ceiling; it does not predict penetration.

The correction is to calibrate the capture rate against branches already open in comparable geographies rather than assuming one — which is possible, because the trade-area work in the defensive track already measures exactly that for every existing location.
