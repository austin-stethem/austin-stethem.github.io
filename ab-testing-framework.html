1. The Problem: Testing Market Resistance

A price change and the volume that follows it are separated by weeks of ordinary variance, which is why nobody could say afterwards whether a change had worked: the signal was there and no method existed to pull it out of the noise. I built the method and put it in front of the people approving the increase beforehand, not after: a test designed once the result is in is not a test.

The Enterprise Scenario (Step 4 of 5) Our deterministic algorithm (Step 2) successfully identified the price floors needed to defend our margins, and our Monte Carlo models (Step 3) confirmed our risk boundaries. However, mathematical theory must be validated by actual market behavior. Before we fully commit, I ran a controlled A/B test on the live B2B portal to find out whether the higher prices are costing us quote-to-order conversion.

Before deploying the Algorithmic Margin Defense engine across the entire network, executive leadership needed empirical proof that dynamic pricing wouldn't erode B2B conversion rates. A mathematical model that protects margins is entirely useless if it causes customers to abandon their shopping carts in protest.

To validate the algorithm safely, I architected a randomized A/B test on our B2B E-Commerce portal. Group A (Control) received our legacy static pricing. Group B (Treatment) received the new dynamic, algorithmic pricing. To separate a real change in conversion from ordinary daily variance, I applied a Two-Proportion Z-Test to the results.

2. Translating Math to Business Strategy

What this deliberately does not do is decide the price. It answers one question for the person who has to approve the change: would we be able to tell if this went wrong? A pilot too small to resolve the effect is worse than no pilot, because it returns a confident-looking number that cannot be distinguished from noise; and that number will end up in a deck. Establishing the threshold before the test runs is the difference between evidence and a story.

The Power of "Inconclusive" Tests

If you look at the simulator's default state, you will notice a massive business advantage hidden inside the math. We raised prices and conversion fell from 10.0% to 9.5%. Sales wanted the increase reverted, which was a fair reading of what they could see, they carry the volume number, and half a point is real money at their end of it.

But the A/B test showed the drop was statistically inconclusive. Because the green curve still overlapped the blue baseline curve, the drop in volume could not be attributed to the price increase. It was within the normal bounds of daily random variance. That is what settled it, and it settled it without anyone having to win on seniority. Sales got something better than being overruled: a test that would have shown a real drop if there had been one. We held the higher prices firm. We generated substantial EBITDA without triggering a measurable exodus of customer volume.

The discipline that makes this defensible: a non-significant result is not proof of no effect, so the decision was paired with a power calculation. We sized the test to detect the smallest volume loss that would have made the price increase EBITDA-negative. Passing that bar is what justified holding; the p-value alone would not have.

This strict adherence to statistical modeling acts as an organizational guardrail. It prevents knee-jerk price drops when sales are slightly sluggish, but it also instantly rings the alarm bell (flashing the "Volume Loss" warning) the moment a price increase actually crosses the mathematical threshold of destroying demand.
