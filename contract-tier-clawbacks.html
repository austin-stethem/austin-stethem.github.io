1. Discounts Are Granted Once and Reviewed Never

Where this sits in the track Every previous step spends something: a building, a rep's day, a phone call. This one spends nothing. The revenue is already flowing; it is simply flowing at a price the account stopped earning some time ago.

Volume tiers are negotiated on a forecast. An account projects annual purchases at a level that justifies a deeper discount, the tier is granted, and the pricing is written into the customer master. What happens next is that the forecast turns out to be wrong, or the business changes, or a fleet downsizes — and the tier stays exactly where it was, because nothing in the system re-tests entitlement.

The result is an account buying at a national-fleet discount on regional-shop volume, for years, entirely inside the rules as written. Nobody did anything wrong. The tier simply outlived the volume that justified it, and no report exists that would surface the mismatch, because the account still looks healthy: it buys consistently, it pays on time, and its revenue is stable.

This is the least glamorous analysis in the track and reliably the highest return, because the recovery requires no new customer, no new product, and no new territory.

2. Testing Entitlement Rather Than Assuming It

The whole test turns on one decision: what counts as the account. Assigned tier lives in the customer master, actual volume lives in the transaction history, and the two do not agree. A customer buying through three ship-to locations is one row in the master and three in the history.

Get that grain wrong and it fails in both directions. Split the volume three ways and every multi-location customer flunks a test it should pass, landing on a list that tells a representative to raise their price. Roll it up too far and a parent inherits volume its subsidiaries earned on separately negotiated terms, so a real overspend vanishes. I roll ship-to records to the billing entity. That is the level the discount was granted at, and it is why the list can go to a sales manager without a caveat attached.

The audit compares two numbers per account: the discount the contract grants, and the discount the trailing volume would earn under the standard tier schedule. Where the contracted discount is deeper than the earned one, the difference multiplied by annual volume is the leakage.

Test Rule Why it is not simply a query
Earned tier trailing 12-month units → tier Trailing volume has to be measured on the same basis as the original commitment. An account committing on units and measured on dollars will drift purely with price changes, and the audit will fire on inflation rather than behaviour.
Entitlement gap contracted − earned Positive values only. An account earning a deeper tier than it holds is under-discounted, which is a different finding entirely and belongs on a different list.
Grace period months below threshold One soft quarter is not a tier change. Without a minimum sustained period the audit fires on seasonality and destroys its own credibility with the sales organisation.
Materiality floor annual leakage > threshold A $400 gap is not worth a conversation that risks a $60,000 account. The floor is what keeps the list short enough to be worked.

Tiers get negotiated on a forecast and then nothing checks them against what actually happened, because the check is not simple: assigned tier and real volume live in different systems that disagree on what an account is. Until somebody resolves that, the comparison returns nonsense and there is nothing to act on. I resolved it, proposed the audit, and proposed the grace period in the same breath: an audit that fires on one soft quarter gets switched off by the first sales manager it embarrasses.

4. Closing the Loop

A clawback list is trivially easy to generate and surprisingly hard to act on, because every row is a relationship somebody in sales owns. The analysis earns its place by arriving with the objection already answered: here is the gap, here is how long it has persisted, here is how exposed the account is, and here is the recommendation with the risk priced in.

It also closes the track. Step 1 mapped where competitors are. That same exposure score, built to identify where they could take business from us, turns out to be the thing that decides whether we can safely reprice our own accounts. The offensive asset and the defensive constraint are the same number read in opposite directions.

The Honest Limitation

Entitlement is only testable where the commitment was recorded in a form the data can check. Tiers granted verbally, granted as a relationship concession, or granted on a basis nobody wrote down cannot be audited — they can only be surfaced as anomalies and referred to whoever remembers the arrangement.

In practice that is a meaningful share of the book, and the audit's real first output is often a list of accounts whose pricing nobody can explain. That is a less satisfying finding than a leakage figure, and a more useful one, because it is the thing that has to be fixed before any of this becomes routine.

End of the Attack Track

Five steps, ordered by what each costs to execute: a building, a lost customer, a conversation, and finally nothing at all. The defensive track runs the same scenario from the other direction — holding margin when a cost shock arrives rather than finding revenue when it does not.
