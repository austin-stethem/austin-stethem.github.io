1. Where Does the Truck Actually Go?

Where this sits in the track Step 1 asked whether the inventory record matches physical reality. Step 2 asked whether two records describe the same entity. This step asks a simpler question that turns out to be harder: where is the customer.

A distribution network runs on delivery. Every branch sends vehicles out on routes, and the shape of those routes determines freight cost, driver hours and how quickly a customer gets a part. None of it appears in the ERP as geography. It appears as a branch identifier and a customer identifier, and the physical relationship between the two is inferred rather than recorded.

Plotting that relationship — a line from every branch to every account it delivers to — produces a map that looks like overlapping spiders. The value is not the picture. It is that the picture makes two things visible that no report shows: how far the average delivery actually travels, and how often two branches are driving into the same town.

2. The Input Problem, Stated Rather Than Hidden

The customer's physical location is not reliably recorded. Branches are not required to populate a ship-to address distinct from the billing address, and in practice they frequently do not — because the drivers already know the routes. From an operational standpoint that is entirely rational. From a mapping standpoint it means a meaningful share of delivery points are geocoded from a billing address, and some of those are post office boxes or corporate accounts payable departments in another town.

This cannot be fixed at source. Asking eighty branches to maintain a field that provides them no operational benefit is a request that will be complied with for about a fortnight. So the correct response is not to pretend the input is clean, but to bound the error and publish the bound.

Control What it does
Proximity shield Any account resolving more than 75 miles from the branch that delivers to it is excluded and the count logged. A branch does not run a daily route 200 miles out; a point that far away is a billing address, not a delivery address.
Postal-box flagging Points geocoded from a post office box are identifiable and can be reported separately, so their contribution to any conclusion is visible rather than blended in.
Published residual The share of points affected is reported alongside the map, so a reader can judge whether the error is material to the question they are asking. For route overlap it is not; for individual account placement it can be.

The Residual, Measured

Across the surviving delivery set, 57.6% of points sit within ten miles of their branch and 88% within twenty-five. Only 0.3% fall in the last five miles before the shield boundary — so the shield is clipping a handful of genuine outliers rather than truncating a long tail that would change the picture.

Roughly 4% of points are geocoded from a postal box. Those sit further out on every measure, with a median distance about three miles greater than street-addressed points. That is the size of the known distortion, and it is small enough that it does not move a conclusion about route overlap, which is what the map is for.

Leadership was told this when the tool was delivered. A map presented as precise would have been wrong; a map presented as bounded, with the bound stated, is usable.

3. Overlap Is Not Automatically Waste

Most towns receive deliveries from more than one branch. Some of that is genuine redundancy and some is stocking depth — a second branch carrying a line the nearest one does not. 4. What the Map Is Allowed to Conclude

A redundant route is not proof of waste. The second branch may stock a product line the nearest one does not, in which case the extra miles buy something. It may serve an account with a contractual relationship to that specific location. It may be covering for a vehicle shortage that resolved months ago and was never revisited.

What the analysis produces is therefore an ordered list of conversations, not a savings figure. The output that gets used is the short version: the towns where a recurring route runs past a nearer branch, ranked by annual exposure, handed to the person who can say in one sentence whether there is a reason.

That framing matters for adoption. An analysis that arrives claiming a dollar saving invites an argument about the dollar. An analysis that arrives saying here are eleven routes I cannot explain, and here is what each costs if the explanation turns out to be nothing invites an answer.
