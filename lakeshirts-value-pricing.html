Moving Lakeshirts from cost-plus to value pricing

Austin Stethem · prepared for Matt Hugdahl and Lakeshirts | Blue 84 · October 2026

Lakeshirts customers pay for speed, quality and service. Cost-plus pricing can't see that. It prices every shirt from what it costs to make, so a rush reorder that saves a resort shop its July season and a core tee a bookstore shops against three catalogs end up with the same markup. Where customers would gladly pay more, the difference goes uncollected and never shows up in a report. Below is how I would find that margin, phase it in safely, and keep it.

How to read it. Section 1 sizes the problem: what cost-plus leaves on the table. Sections 2 to 4 are the cost side: where the unit cost in section 1 comes from, what a shirt really earns once every deduction is taken, and how much an order's size and destination change that cost. Sections 5 to 7 are the price side: tiering customers on how they buy, moving prices from cost-plus toward value a step at a time, and holding the new prices once they're in. Every table pulls from the same four product lines and the same costs. Change the markup or the daily volume in section 1 and every section follows.

Sample data throughout, built to show the method. The only Lakeshirts figure is the 35,000 garments a day from the job posting. On real invoice and cost data, the same models run in Python.

The problem

1.Where cost-plus leaves margin on the table

Today's price is cost-plus: unit cost times a markup. A value price starts instead from the market reference, the price a customer would compare against (a competitor's quote or a catalog), adds a service premium for what speed, quality and reliability are worth on that program, and never drops below unit cost. The gap between the two is what cost-plus leaves on the table, or, on K-12, where it overprices.

K-12 comes out the other way: cost-plus prices it above what that market carries. Value pricing finds both.

The cost side

2.The cost floor: from landed blank to unit cost

Before any price is set, the floor itself should be as low as it can honestly go. The landed blank is what a blank garment costs once it reaches Detroit Lakes: the supplier's price, less what shopping suppliers against each other and collecting vendor rebates wins back, plus tariffs and inbound freight. Putting those last two into the landed cost means they get recovered in the price instead of quietly eating margin.

The landed blank is the first line of the unit cost. Ink, labor and overhead are added per piece, then two allowances: an average setup cost per piece (the press time lost changing jobs) and an average outbound freight cost per piece (shipping the finished order to the customer), both figured at a 640-piece order shipped to Zone 3. That unit cost, times the markup, is today's cost-plus price in section 1.

3.What a shirt actually earns after the waterfall

The list price is only the start. Between it and the money that lands sits the price waterfall: the volume break for order size, program terms (the discount written into a customer's contract), a freight credit given back to the customer, co-op (marketing money given to the customer) and returns. What is left is the pocket price, and margin should be judged on that. The volume break here is the average across the order-size ladder in section 4. The freight credit is separate from the two freights Lakeshirts pays itself: bringing blanks in (section 2) and shipping orders out (section 4).

4.What it costs to fill an order: setup and freight

The unit cost in section 2 carries setup and freight as averages. This section shows what those averages hide. Every job stops the press: changing blanks, burning and mounting screens, mixing ink and registering the press all cost money while nothing prints. Spread across a 10,000-piece run, that changeover is pennies a shirt. On a 24-piece order it can cost more than the shirt itself. Freight works the same way, so I would zone customers in belts by distance from Detroit Lakes, the way the postal service does, and price from each zone's average freight.

The price side

5.Tiering customers by how they buy

Once the cost to serve an order is visible, customers can be tiered on what actually drives it: annual volume, how often they order, how big each order runs, where it ships and what they buy. The tier sets the terms. Volume is rewarded with a rebate paid after it's earned rather than a discount given up front. Small orders carry their own setup through a small-order fee. Distant zones carry their own freight. Fleece, where the margin dollars per piece are largest, gets a little extra push.

6.Phasing it in, with cost as the floor

Prices move from today's cost-plus price toward the value price in section 1, a step at a time, capped each quarter so no customer sees a jump, and never below unit cost. Volume responds by program: at a sensitivity of 1.0x, a 1% price increase costs about 1% of volume on the college tee, less than half that on the resort tee, where service decides the order. The sensitivity is an assumption to be replaced by what the pilot shows.

7.Keeping the gains with a price corridor

A new price only counts if customers actually pay something close to it. Left alone, one-off quotes pull it down a deal at a time. A price corridor is a floor set a few percent under each tier price: inside it, a rep can quote freely; below it, the quote needs a sign-off and a reason code. At Arnold, one high-volume part had more than 400 distinct transacted prices against an eight-tier ladder. The first 90 days

  1. Baseline. Rebuild landed cost, the waterfall and pocket margin by program and customer from 12 months of invoices.
  2. Cost to serve. Measure changeover time by job type and set freight zones from actual shipping spend.
  3. Segment. Tier accounts by volume, order size, frequency, zone and garment mix, with Sales and Customer Service in the room.
  4. Pilot one program. Move one program toward value, watch volume and reorders weekly, and adjust.
  5. Govern and expand. Set corridors and an approval path below the floor, then roll out program by program.
