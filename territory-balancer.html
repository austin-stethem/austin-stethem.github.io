1. Who Owns the Account?

Where this sits in the track The previous step asked where the customer physically is. This one asks who is responsible for them — a question the system answers confidently and, in about a thousand towns, answers more than once.

Sales assignment is recorded as a field on the customer master. One account, one representative, unambiguous. That field is correct in the sense that it contains a value, and it is the basis for commission, for reporting, and for who gets a call when something goes wrong.

What it does not encode is geography. Assignments accumulate over years: a representative picks up an account because they knew the owner, or covered for somebody, or because the account was small when it was assigned and is not small any more. Each individual decision was sensible. The aggregate is a coverage map that nobody designed and nobody can see, because the customer master lists assignments one row at a time and no report renders them as territory.

Mapping every assigned account against its representative's hub makes three things visible at once, none of which appear in any existing report.

2. Three Questions the Assignment Field Cannot Answer

Radius discipline Distance from each account to its representative's hub A long-distance assignment costs windscreen time that produces nothing. It usually exists because of a relationship, which makes it defensible; but it should be a deliberate exception rather than an accident nobody has looked at.
Coverage conflict Towns where several representatives hold accounts Two representatives in one town is normal. Nine is a signal that the assignment logic has stopped corresponding to anything. It also means several people are driving to the same place on different days.

The Distribution Is the Finding

Across the mapped book, the median representative carries roughly a hundred accounts and the largest carries three times that. What matters is not the extremes but the middle: only about one representative in four sits within twenty-five percent of the median. The other three quarters are meaningfully over or under.

That is not a few outliers to correct. It is the absence of a target — and it is what you get when assignments are made one at a time, correctly, for twenty years, with nothing aggregating them.

4. Capacity: What Is Mathematically Serviceable

Workload imbalance describes a distribution. It does not answer the question a sales director actually has, which is whether the books at the top of that distribution can be covered at all.

That question has an arithmetic answer and does not require anybody's opinion. A representative makes a certain number of calls in a day. A visit cycle specifies how often each account should be seen. Multiply those out and the maximum serviceable book falls out of it:

Maximum serviceable book calls per day × working days per week × cycle length in weeks

At eight calls a day on a six-week cycle, one representative can see 240 accounts once each. A book of 285 is not a stretch target — it is 45 accounts that will not be visited in the cycle, and the arithmetic says so before anyone looks at performance.

Run that across the field and the output is not a judgement about any individual. It is a count of accounts that sit beyond anyone's capacity to service on the stated cycle; and those accounts are where revenue quietly decays, because nothing in the reporting distinguishes an account nobody visited from an account that simply bought less.

Whether the field is fully covered is not a matter of effort or intent — it is a function of two numbers that leadership sets, often without realising they are setting them. Decide that every account deserves a visit every four weeks and a fifth of the book becomes uncoverable overnight. Decide on six weeks and the problem nearly disappears. Neither decision is currently being made explicitly, which is how a coverage policy ends up being an accident.

Why Hand-Managed Assignment Degrades on Its Own

No individual assignment causes this. Each one is made for a good reason: a representative knows the owner, covers a vacancy, inherits a retiring colleague's book, or picks up an account that was small at the time. What no single decision can account for is the aggregate, because the aggregate is not visible to anyone making the decisions.

Left alone, the drift has three predictable outputs. Representatives become frustrated, because carrying twice a peer's book for the same expectations is legible to the person carrying it long before it is legible to management. Accounts go unserved, invisibly, at the bottom of the largest books. And geographic coverage frays, because assignments made one at a time never optimise for travel, so two representatives end up crossing each other's paths in the same town on different days.

All three are consequences of process rather than of people, and all three are measurable the moment the assignments are rendered against geography instead of listed as rows. That is the whole intervention, not a reorganisation, but a report that did not previously exist.

5. Coverage Conflict, and Why It Is Not Automatically Wrong

I built this to be handed to a sales director, which rules out what it might otherwise have done. It does not propose reassignments. Accounts are not interchangeable: a relationship that took four years is not a row to be moved because a total came out uneven; and a model that suggests moves it cannot justify gets dismissed along with the finding underneath it. So the output is the size of the gap and the accounts involved. The judgment stays with the person who knows the relationships. The capacity arithmetic is included for the same reason: it makes the constraint arguable, not asserted.

Roughly a thousand towns in the mapped book have accounts held by more than one representative. The distribution has a long tail: most have two, a substantial number have three or four, and a handful have nine or more.

Two representatives in one town is usually correct. Accounts differ — a heavy-duty fleet and a two-bay independent are different sales conversations, and specialisation beats geography. The signal is not the existence of overlap but its depth. When a town reaches five or six representatives, the assignments have almost certainly stopped tracking any deliberate segmentation and started reflecting the order in which accounts happened to be picked up.

The output is therefore ranked by depth rather than flagged by existence, and the towns at the top of that list are worth an hour of a sales manager's attention each. Not to be reassigned automatically — to be explained, and reassigned only where no explanation exists.

Why Ownership Belongs in a Data Governance Track

Territory looks like a sales management topic rather than a master data one, and that is exactly why it drifts. The assignment field is never wrong in a way that fails validation: every account has exactly one representative, the referential integrity is perfect, and no report will ever flag it.

It is wrong in the only way that matters: the single recorded owner is, in about a thousand towns, one of several people actually calling on that customer. A field can be internally consistent and still not describe reality, and no amount of database discipline surfaces that. Only rendering it against geography does.
