Lost Account Win-Back

1. Nobody Files a Report When a Customer Leaves

Where this sits in the track Step 2 costs a building. This step costs a phone call to somebody who already knows the company, already has an account number, and already knows how the delivery works.

Business-to-business customers rarely announce a departure. There is no cancellation, no exit interview, no field in the system that flips from active to lost. Orders get smaller, then less frequent, then stop; and because nothing in the ERP changes state, the account sits in the customer master looking exactly like an account that simply had a quiet quarter.

The consequence is that most organisations cannot answer the question “who did we lose last year, and what were they worth?” without somebody reconstructing it by hand. Which means the cheapest available growth: a customer who has already bought, already been onboarded, and already had their pricing set up — is invisible.

2. The Label Is Already in the Transaction History

Leaving has a signature. An account that stopped buying has a revenue series that decays, and one that switched suppliers has a series that decays sharply and then flatlines. That signature is enough to classify accounts without collecting anything new — the only requirement is a transaction extract that reaches further back than the trailing twelve months most reports default to.

The first decision was how far back the window has to reach. Standard reporting stops at twelve months, which is exactly long enough to hide the thing being looked for: an account that left fourteen months ago has no opening quarter to decline from, so it reads as a quiet small account rather than a loss. I went at the transaction tables directly to get a longer series.

Two things then decide whether the labels mean anything, and neither shows up in the output. An account that changed customer ID mid-period reads as one departure and one arrival: a churn that never happened and a win that never happened, both entering the list as real. And a quarter carrying one large return reads as a collapse. Both are resolved before a label is assigned, because every downstream number inherits them silently: the ranking, the recovery odds, and the validation set that is supposed to be checking the exposure scoring from Step 1.

Comparing an account's opening quarter against its most recent quarter produces four states:

Label Signature What it means commercially
Hard churnlast quarter = 0Gone. Almost certainly buying from somebody else, because the vehicles they service did not stop needing parts.
Soft churndecline ≥ 60%Still buying, but the majority of the wallet has moved. Usually a competitor took the fast-moving lines and left the awkward ones.
At riskdecline ≥ 25%Something changed. Worth a call before it becomes one of the two rows above.
Retainedwithin normal variationNo action. Included so the classifier has a negative class, which is what makes it testable.

That last row matters more than it looks. A labelled set containing both churned and retained accounts is a validation set, and it is the thing that lets the competitive-threat scoring in Step 1 be checked rather than believed. If accounts that actually left do not score higher on competitive exposure than accounts that stayed, the exposure weighting is wrong — and that is discoverable before anybody acts on it.

3. Ranking the Recoverable

No one asked for a churn list, because the company did not know it was missing one — those accounts were still in the master and still looked active. I derived the labels instead of waiting for a system that was never going to record them.

Not every lost account is worth chasing. Recovery probability falls as the gap since the last order widens, so the ranking is expected recoverable gross profit, value multiplied by the odds, rather than raw value.

4. What the Ranking Changes

A raw list of lost accounts sorted by size sends a representative to the biggest name first, which is usually the one that has been gone longest and is hardest to recover. Sorting on expected value inverts that: a mid-sized account that went quiet four months ago outranks a large one that left two years ago, because the odds difference is larger than the value difference.

The thresholds are exposed deliberately. There is no correct answer to what counts as churn: a 30% decline is catastrophic for a steady industrial account and normal for a seasonal agricultural one. Making the boundary adjustable turns an argument about definitions into an argument about one number, which is a much shorter conversation.

The Honest Limitation

A revenue decline says an account stopped buying. It does not say why. A shop that closed, a fleet that downsized, and a customer a competitor took all produce the same series, and only the third is recoverable. Roughly speaking the first two are noise in this list.

Two corrections narrow it. The competitive exposure score from Step 1 distinguishes accounts that sat inside a contested pocket from those that did not: a decline in a contested pocket is far more likely to be a defection than a closure. And a representative can disqualify a row in one call, which is precisely why the output is a short ranked list rather than a dashboard.
