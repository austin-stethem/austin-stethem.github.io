The finding this portfolio is built on

A ladder with eight tiers should produce eight prices.

I pulled the rolling twelve-month history for one high-volume part and found 412 distinct transacted prices. Nobody had measured the dispersion, because no report existed that would show it — so I wrote one, in an afternoon.

What I am for

The middle seat

Enough finance to ask for the right thing. Enough engineering to hear why it cannot be done that way — and to work out what would.

The work I want is the tangled kind. Someone hands over a system that has grown for twenty years without anyone maintaining the logic underneath it, and the job is to work out what it actually does before deciding what it should do.

I build the logic, then the prototype, then run the board that gets it delivered. Specifying what a thing must calculate, validating that it does, and driving it to done is one job to me rather than three.

Across the current network I work with accounting, sales, marketing, IT, logistics and product. The pricing and master data layer touches all six, and nothing below shipped without satisfying more than one of them.

Away from work it is grand strategy and factory-building games, which is the same instinct at lower stakes: find the constraint, rebuild whatever is throttling everything downstream, and refuse to accept that the efficient version has to be the ugly one.

Sequence

How it unfolded

None of this was planned as a portfolio. Each piece exists because the one before it uncovered something that had to be dealt with.

Arrival

An ERP nobody could fully explain

Root-level pricing access, 565+ manufacturer lines, and a tier structure that had accreted for two decades without anyone maintaining the logic underneath it.

The flood

The warehouse goes under, the database does not know

Inventory records kept promising stock that was underwater. Reconciling physical reality against the ERP kept the network transacting through it.

Consequence

The data could not answer basic questions

Entities with two names, customers geocoded to post office boxes, accounts with several owners. Five failure modes, each producing a plausible number a competent analyst would build on.

The measurement

412 prices for one part

With the foundation trustworthy, dispersion became measurable for the first time. One part, twelve months, and a spread covering everything from near break-even to full retail.

Response

Ten models, two directions

Five to defend margin when costs move. Five to find revenue when they do not. All of them resting on the foundation work that came first.

How it was built

Method, plainly

This site is itself a delivered project: three workstreams, fifteen scoped deliverables, several hundred revisions, each specified, reviewed and closed. If you want evidence that I can run work to done, it is the thing you are reading.

Authorship

The interactive tools were built by directing an LLM, not by hand-coding them. The specification, the domain logic, the validation and the decision to accept are mine. Generating a Monte Carlo simulation is inexpensive now; knowing what it ought to produce, and catching it when it does not, is not.

The data

Every figure inside the dashboards is synthetic. Structures, ratios and failure modes come from production systems; the numbers are proxies. Competitors, customers and branches are fictional and consistent across pages, so an account in one case study is the same account in the next.

What is not claimed

Several models here are in scale testing rather than running the business. Where I could not measure an outcome I have said so rather than estimated one — including on the page where a dramatic number was available and would not have survived the follow-up question.

The standard

A model that is correct and unreadable has failed. So has a readable one that is wrong. Refusing to choose between those is why this site carries several hundred revisions rather than a dozen — and why every correction found since launch stays in the git history, including the ones where a finding I was confident about turned out to be a deliberate decision somebody had made for good reasons.
