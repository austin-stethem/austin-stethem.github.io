The finding this portfolio is built on

A ladder with eight tiers should produce eight prices.

I pulled the rolling twelve-month history for one high-volume part and found more than four hundred distinct transacted prices. Nobody had measured the dispersion, because no report existed that would show it; so I wrote one, in an afternoon.

What I am for

The middle seat

Enough finance to ask for the right thing, enough engineering to hear why it cannot be done that way, and enough data discipline to know which answer will hold.

The work I want is the tangled kind. Someone hands over a system that has grown for twenty years without anyone maintaining the logic underneath it, and the job is to work out what it actually does before deciding what it should do.

I build the logic, then the prototype, then run the board that gets it delivered. Specifying what a thing must calculate, validating that it does, and driving it to done is one job to me, not three.

Across the current network I work with accounting, sales, marketing, IT, logistics and product. The pricing and master data layer touches all six, and nothing below shipped without satisfying more than one of them.

Away from work it is grand strategy and factory-building games, which is the same instinct at lower stakes: find the constraint, rebuild whatever is throttling everything downstream, and refuse to accept that the efficient version has to be the ugly one.

What that year involved

The city went under. The distribution centre that supplied every branch flooded, and was eighteen days from serving them again. Holding margin through that, and through the cost movement of the years either side of it, is the reason any of the work above exists; and the reason it is framed as defence rather than optimisation.

Eighty branches sell what the pricing layer lets them sell.

A tier structure that quietly gives away margin is not an abstraction to the people working those counters. It is their commission, then their hours, and eventually their store.

Sequence

How it unfolded

None of this was planned as a portfolio. Each piece answered what the last one exposed.

Arrival

Twenty years of accumulated pricing logic

This was the first job out of college, and it began in the supply-chain aftermath of the pandemic, with vendor costs moving faster than anyone could reprice against. What I was handed was root-level pricing access, 565+ manufacturer lines, and a tier structure two decades in the making: every decision sensible when it was made, none of it ever reviewed as a whole.

The years since

Cost shock as the normal condition

Successive tariff rounds, and then their unwind. The unwind is the part people miss: a lifted tariff does not restore a cost. Supply has to catch up, freight has to renormalise, surcharges come off on their own schedule — so the shock keeps arriving for months or years after the policy that caused it has reversed. Every model in the Defense track exists because of that lag.

The flood

The warehouse goes under, the database does not know

Inventory records kept promising stock that was underwater. Reconciling physical reality against the ERP kept the network transacting through it.

Consequence

The data could not answer basic questions

Entities with two names, customers geocoded to post office boxes, accounts with several owners. Five failure modes, each producing a plausible number a competent analyst would build on.

The measurement

Hundreds of prices for one part

With the foundation trustworthy, dispersion became measurable for the first time. One part, twelve months, and a spread covering everything from near break-even to full retail.

Response

Fifteen models, three tracks

Five to defend margin when costs move. Five to find revenue when they do not. And five on the layer underneath both, because a model is only as honest as the extract feeding it.

How it was built

Method, plainly

This site is itself a delivered project: three workstreams, fifteen scoped deliverables, several hundred revisions, each specified, reviewed and closed. If you want evidence that I can run work to done, it is the thing you are reading.

Where the work actually is

Not in the code. It is knowing which tables hold the truth, how they join, and which rows are corrupt. The SQL is mine. The application layer around it I specified, directed and validated instead of hand-writing, and I run the environment and the deploys.

What is claimed, and what is not

Several of these models are in scale testing, not running the business. Where I could not measure an outcome I said so instead of estimating one. A model that is correct and unreadable has failed, and so has a readable one that is wrong.

The data, and what is withheld

Every figure in the dashboards is synthetic; the structures and failure modes are real. Findings in prose are stated as ratios or rounded magnitudes. No price, cost, margin, customer name or vendor term appears on this site.

Who these were for

Every model went to somebody who had to act on it: a pricing manager approving a floor, a sales director weighing a rebalance. That limits what a tool may claim. One that overstates what it knows gets dismissed once, and takes the finding with it.
