1. A Missing Category Is a Supplier You Cannot See

Where this sits in the track Step 3 chased customers who left. This step looks at customers who never left and are still buying somewhere else — which is the cheapest revenue in the entire track, because the account, the pricing and the delivery route already exist.

An account that buys brake pads every month and has never bought a rotor is not expressing a preference. Pads and rotors are replaced together; a shop doing brake work is buying rotors from somebody. The absence is not a gap in demand, it is a competitor with a foothold nobody has noticed.

Finding those gaps by intuition does not scale past a handful of obvious pairings, and intuition tends to produce pairs that are merely common rather than genuinely linked. Oil filters sell to almost everyone; pairing them with anything looks impressive and means nothing. Distinguishing a real relationship from two independently popular products is precisely what association rule metrics exist to do.

2. Three Numbers, and Only One of Them Matters

A rule of the form “accounts buying A also buy B” is scored on three quantities. The first two are routinely quoted and routinely mislead; the third is the one that decides whether a rule is worth acting on.

Metric Definition What it does and does not tell you
Support P(A and B) How often the pair occurs at all. Guards against building a strategy on six accounts, and nothing more. High support just means both products are popular.
Confidence P(B | A) Of the accounts buying A, the share that also buy B. Intuitive and dangerous: if 80% of all accounts buy B, then 80% confidence means the rule has told you nothing.
Lift P(B | A) ÷ P(B) Confidence divided by the base rate of B. Lift of 1.0 means A and B are independent — buying A tells you nothing about B. Above 1.0 means a genuine relationship. This is the only one of the three that survives the popularity problem.

The practical consequence: a rule can have high confidence and be worthless. If nearly every account buys filters, then “buys oil → buys filters” will show strong confidence while carrying lift below 1.0, meaning oil buyers are actually less likely to buy filters than the average account. Acting on confidence alone sends representatives to sell things customers were going to buy anyway.

4. What Makes This Actionable Rather Than Interesting

Association rule mining produces a lot of output, most of which is unusable. Three constraints turn it into something a sales organisation can work.

  • Rules are filtered on lift and ranked on money. Lift decides whether a relationship is real; the size of the account decides whether it is worth a visit. Ranking on lift alone surfaces strong relationships between two categories nobody buys much of.
  • The output is per-account, not per-rule. A representative cannot act on “brakes imply rotors, lift 2.1”. They can act on a named account, a category it already buys, and a category it does not.
  • Estimated value uses the account's own volume. The opportunity at a large fleet and a two-bay independent are not the same size, even for the same rule. Sizing from the account's existing spend in the trigger category keeps the ranking honest.

The Honest Limitation

A gap is not always a competitor. An account may not buy rotors because it sends brake work out, because it is contractually supplied by a fleet programme, or because the category simply does not apply to the vehicles it services. The model cannot distinguish those from a genuine opening.

What it does do is reduce thousands of accounts to a short, ordered list where the hit rate is high enough to justify the calls — and a representative disqualifies a bad row in one conversation. The measure of the tool is not precision, it is whether the list beats calling in account-number order. It does, comfortably.
